Understanding Thalassemia

Learn about this inherited blood disorder, the importance of medical care, and how voluntary blood donation can support people who need regular transfusions.

1. What Is Thalassemia?

Thalassemia is an inherited blood disorder that affects the body's ability to produce haemoglobin. Haemoglobin is a protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body.

When the body does not produce enough healthy haemoglobin, it may produce fewer healthy red blood cells. This can lead to anaemia, which means the body has difficulty supplying enough oxygen to its tissues.

Thalassemia varies in severity. Some people have few or no symptoms, while others need regular medical monitoring and specialised treatment throughout life.

Thalassemia is inherited. It is not contagious and cannot spread from one person to another through everyday contact.

2. Causes and Inheritance

Thalassemia is caused by changes in genes involved in making haemoglobin. These genetic changes are passed from parents to children.

A person may inherit a thalassemia-related gene change from one parent and become a carrier. Carriers often have no symptoms or may have mild anaemia. A carrier can pass the gene change to their children.

The chance of a child having a more severe form depends on the specific gene changes carried by both parents. Genetic counselling and appropriate testing can help families understand their individual risks.

3. Types of Thalassemia

The two main groups are alpha thalassemia and beta thalassemia. The type and severity depend on which haemoglobin genes are affected.

Alpha Thalassemia

Occurs when changes affect genes responsible for producing alpha-globin, a component of haemoglobin. Severity ranges from a carrier state to serious disease.

Beta Thalassemia

Occurs when changes affect genes responsible for producing beta-globin. It can range from a mild carrier state to a severe condition requiring regular treatment.

Common severity categories

  • Thalassemia trait or minor: Often causes no symptoms or only mild anaemia.
  • Thalassemia intermedia: May cause moderate symptoms and require treatment depending on the individual.
  • Thalassemia major: A severe form that commonly requires regular blood transfusions and specialist care.

These terms are commonly used for beta thalassemia. A clinician determines the exact diagnosis and severity using appropriate tests.

4. Signs and Symptoms

Symptoms depend on the type and severity of thalassemia. Some carriers feel well and may discover their carrier status only during a blood test.

Possible signs include:

  • Feeling tired or weak.
  • Pale or yellowish skin.
  • Shortness of breath, particularly with activity.
  • Slow growth in children with more severe disease.
  • Changes in the spleen or other organs in some cases.
  • Bone changes in certain severe, untreated cases.
These symptoms can have many causes. They do not confirm thalassemia on their own. Medical evaluation and appropriate blood tests are needed.

5. How Is Thalassemia Diagnosed?

Healthcare professionals use medical history, examination and laboratory tests to identify thalassemia and determine its type.

  • Complete blood count (CBC): Measures red blood cell characteristics and helps identify patterns associated with anaemia.
  • Haemoglobin analysis: Tests such as haemoglobin electrophoresis or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) help identify haemoglobin patterns.
  • Genetic testing: May be recommended to identify specific gene changes.
  • Family screening: May help identify carrier status when recommended by a healthcare professional.

6. Treatment and Medical Care

Treatment depends on the type and severity of thalassemia. People with mild forms may need only periodic monitoring, while more severe forms need ongoing care from a specialist.

Possible treatments include:

  • Blood transfusions: Replace red blood cells when clinically needed.
  • Iron chelation therapy: Helps remove excess iron that can build up after repeated transfusions.
  • Monitoring: Regular tests help healthcare teams monitor blood counts, iron levels and organ health.
  • Specialist treatment: Some patients may be eligible for stem cell transplantation or other advanced therapies.

Treatment plans are individualised. Patients should not start iron supplements or change prescribed treatment without medical advice.

7. Why Are Blood Transfusions Important?

Some people with severe thalassemia cannot produce enough healthy red blood cells to meet the body's needs. Regular transfusions can help maintain haemoglobin levels and support normal growth, activity and overall health.

Transfusions must be arranged and monitored by qualified healthcare professionals. Blood is carefully tested and matched to reduce the risk of complications.

Not everyone with thalassemia needs regular transfusions. The decision depends on the person's diagnosis and medical condition.

8. The Role of Blood Donation

Voluntary blood donors help maintain a safe and reliable blood supply for patients who need transfusions, including some people living with thalassemia.

How can donors help?

  • Participate in authorised blood donation drives.
  • Encourage accurate information about donation.
  • Follow the instructions of qualified blood-bank staff.
  • Support awareness activities in schools and communities.
  • Help reduce myths and stigma around blood donation.

Every donation must meet blood-bank safety requirements. Eligibility is assessed by trained staff, and blood is tested before it is used.

9. Screening and Prevention

Because thalassemia is inherited, it cannot be prevented in the same way as an infectious disease. However, awareness and carrier screening can help people understand their genetic status and make informed family-planning decisions.

  • Seek medical advice about carrier screening.
  • Consider genetic counselling when recommended.
  • Discuss test results with a qualified clinician.
  • Learn about available screening options in your area.

Screening recommendations vary according to personal and family history and local medical guidance.

10. Living With Thalassemia

People living with thalassemia can benefit from consistent medical care, clear information and emotional support. Their needs differ depending on the type and severity of their condition.

  • Attend recommended medical appointments.
  • Follow the treatment plan provided by the care team.
  • Discuss fatigue or new symptoms with a clinician.
  • Seek support from family, counsellors or patient groups.
  • Use reliable medical sources to learn about the condition.

11. Frequently Asked Questions

Is thalassemia contagious?

No. It is an inherited genetic condition and does not spread through touching, sharing food or everyday contact.

Can a person with thalassemia donate blood?

Eligibility depends on the person's condition, haemoglobin level and applicable blood-bank rules. The blood-bank medical team makes the final decision.

Does every patient need blood transfusions?

No. Some people have mild forms and need only monitoring, while others require regular transfusions.

Can thalassemia be diagnosed with one symptom?

No. Diagnosis requires appropriate medical assessment and laboratory testing.

Why is awareness important?

Awareness can encourage timely diagnosis, informed screening decisions, access to care and voluntary blood donation for patients who need transfusions.

Be Part of the Change

Learning about thalassemia is one way to support patients and their families. Share reliable information, encourage appropriate screening, and support authorised blood donation programmes.
